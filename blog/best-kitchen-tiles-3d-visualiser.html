Start with the kitchen you already have

The best kitchen tiles bring your cabinets, worktops and lighting together. A beautiful tile in a showroom can look very different beside warm oak doors, cool grey paint or a strongly veined worktop. Start with those fixed elements, then decide whether the tiles should quietly connect them or become the feature.

For many kitchens, a stone-effect porcelain floor and a glazed ceramic splashback are a useful starting combination. The floor creates a calm foundation; the splashback gives you space to introduce colour. Use the 3D kitchen above to test that balance before narrowing down physical samples.

These are design starting points, not rules. If you love a bold floor, keep the splashback quieter. If your worktop has dramatic movement, try a plain splashback first so the two surfaces have room to work together.

Porcelain, ceramic or natural stone?

Porcelain: a practical floor starting point

Porcelain offers a wide choice of stone, marble, terrazzo and timber effects. Choose a product explicitly rated for kitchen floors and assess its cleaning requirements and slip performance. A matt appearance alone does not establish how slip resistant a tile is. The visualiser lets you compare the appearance of these effects; the real product specification determines suitability.

Ceramic: colour for the splashback

Glazed ceramic gives you plenty of scope for colour and a classic rectangular or square format. It is a useful option for splashbacks, especially when you want contrast against a quieter floor. Wall-only tiles must not be used on a floor. Near a hob, confirm the tile, backing and adhesive system are appropriate for the appliance clearances and heat exposure.

Natural stone: character with a care routine

Real marble, limestone and other stones bring individual variation. Their sealing, cleaning and maintenance needs depend on the stone and finish. Acidic spills can mark some stones, so discuss the practical requirements before choosing. Marble-effect porcelain is an alternative when you want veining with a different maintenance profile. The marble option in our 3D tool represents porcelain, not natural marble.

What size tiles work best in a kitchen?

A 600 × 600 mm floor tile is a useful middle ground to explore. A 1200 × 600 mm tile creates fewer joints, but the room dimensions, doorways and cabinet positions determine whether the cuts look balanced. Small kitchens can use large tiles successfully; check the proposed layout rather than assuming smaller rooms need smaller tiles.

For splashbacks, try 200 × 100 mm rectangles for a classic look, 150 × 150 mm squares for a compact rhythm, or 600 × 300 mm tiles for fewer joints. Switch between horizontal and vertical direction in the visualiser to see how the proportions change. Large tiles still need suitable joints and a properly prepared background.

Choose colour and grout together

Ivory, sand and muted warm tones can soften white cabinetry. Sage brings a gentle colour accent, while charcoal gives stronger contrast. Terracotta tones can work well with timber and warm metal fittings. Look at samples in morning light, evening light and under the lighting you will actually use.

Similar-coloured grout makes a tiled surface feel more continuous; contrasting grout makes the grid more noticeable. Consider the amount of joint cleaning you want to do, as well as the appearance. In the 3D tool, matching, warm-white and contrasting grout are deliberately easy to compare.

How to use the 3D kitchen visualiser

  1. Open the 3D view and choose a cabinet colour close to your own.
  2. Adjust the floor colour, tile type and dimensions first.
  3. Choose a splashback colour, type, size and direction.
  4. Compare grout colours, then rotate or use the front and overhead views.
  5. Save a look, change your choices and compare. Select “Get a quote for this look” to include the combination in your enquiry.

You can combine colours and effects freely to explore a mood. Actual collections may not offer every combination shown. The kitchen dimensions stay fixed so you can compare tile scale consistently; this tool does not calculate a purchase quantity.

Turn the idea into a kitchen that works

Before ordering, confirm the tile layout, subfloor condition, surface preparation, movement joints, transitions and any underfloor heating requirements with your installer. Take measurements and allow for cuts and the chosen layout. Our tile calculator can help with an initial quantity estimate; a final take-off should reflect your actual room.

Your questions, answered

Should kitchen floor and wall tiles match?

They can match, but they do not need to. Coordinating undertones often matters more than choosing the identical tile. Start with one main feature and let the other surface support it.

Can I use large tiles in a small kitchen?

Yes, if the layout and cuts suit the space. Check the positions of cabinet edges and thresholds, and confirm that the background can be prepared for the tile format.

Can I preview my actual kitchen here?

This tool uses a fixed sample kitchen. It helps you compare finishes and tile proportions; it does not reconstruct a room from a photo. Send your room photos and measurements with your enquiry for project-specific advice.
